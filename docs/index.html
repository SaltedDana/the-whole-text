Why it works

The architecture of the text

Spoken aloud, a text has a shape. The voice rises on the main point, pauses before a contrast, slows down on a list. Print flattens all of it.

The reader gives that shape back. Opposing sides sit face to face, options sit side by side, steps carry numbers, conclusions stand large. Like a relief map, the peaks rise above the plain, and you see where you are in the terrain.

Built on how people learn

A map comes before the text, so you know what is coming (an advance organizer). The structure words carry visible signals (signaling). The text is grouped into parts (chunking). Each signal is shown by symbol, color and position together (dual coding).

These ideas come from learning research. The reader applies them to the structure of your text and to nothing else.

The whole text, untouched

No summary. No rewriting. No AI judgment about what the text means. Every word is the writer's word.

The model only points to where a connecting word sits and what job it does. Code checks every pointer against the original and drops any that does not match. The instructions the model receives are in the open source code. The one thing a model writes is the short map before the text, and it is labeled.

For every kind of reader

Made for readers with ADHD, ADD or dyslexia, and for anyone who loses the thread of a long text.

It also helps when you read on a phone, in a second language, or at the end of a long day.

Where you need the full, true text

Some texts cannot be summarized, because a skipped condition has a cost.

  • Science papers, methods, supplements
  • Medicine guidelines, consent forms, patient information
  • Law contracts, rulings, terms of service
  • Insurance policies, where the exclusions sit
  • Finance regulations, audit reports, filings
  • Government permits, public notices, tax rules
  • Safety manuals, procedures, standards
  • Education textbooks, exam rules, grant guidelines
  • Home and work leases, employment agreements
